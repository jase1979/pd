## Telling hardwoods and softwoods apart

The names are misleading: "hardwood" and "softwood" describe the **type of tree**, not how hard the timber is. Balsa is a hardwood but is one of the softest timbers there is, while some softwoods such as yew are very hard.

<div class="key-term" markdown="1">
**Hardwood** — timber from **deciduous** (broad-leaved) trees, which usually lose their leaves in autumn. They grow slowly, so hardwoods are generally more expensive.
</div>

<div class="key-term" markdown="1">
**Softwood** — timber from **coniferous** trees, which have needles and cones and are usually evergreen. They grow quickly and straight, so softwoods are cheaper and more readily available.
</div>

| | Hardwoods | Softwoods |
|---|---|---|
| Tree type | Deciduous, broad leaves | Coniferous, needles and cones |
| Growth | Slow — often 60–100+ years before felling | Fast — often 25–30 years |
| Cost | Generally more expensive | Generally cheaper |
| Grain | Often close and decorative | Often wider, with visible knots |
| Sustainability | Slower to replace; tropical hardwoods raise deforestation concerns | Easier to replant and manage in sustainable forests |
| Examples | Beech, balsa, oak, mahogany | Scots pine, Parana pine, spruce, cedar |

**How to tell them apart in practice:** look at the leaves or needles of the tree if you can. On a cut piece, softwoods usually have pale, resinous wood with clear annual rings and knots, while hardwoods often have a finer, more even grain. Weight and hardness are **not** reliable tests.

## Natural wood compared with man-made boards

<div class="key-term" markdown="1">
**Manufactured board** — a man-made sheet material made from layers, particles or fibres of wood bonded together with adhesive under heat and pressure.
</div>

| | Natural timber | Manufactured boards |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Limited by the width of the tree | Large, flat sheets (typically 2440 × 1220 mm) |
| Stability | Can warp, twist, shrink and split as moisture changes | Very stable — little or no movement |
| Grain | Attractive natural grain, but weaker across the grain | No grain direction (MDF) or crossed grain (plywood), so strength is even |
| Defects | Knots, splits and shakes | Uniform and consistent, with no natural defects |
| Appearance | Valued for its natural beauty | Plain; often veneered, laminated or painted |
| Cost | Higher, especially hardwoods | Lower; makes use of waste and low-grade timber |
| Sustainability | Renewable if sourced from managed forests (e.g. FSC) | Uses offcuts and recycled wood, but the adhesives can contain formaldehyde |

Natural timber must be **seasoned** — dried in the air or in a kiln — before use. Reducing the moisture content stops it shrinking, warping and splitting later.

## Aesthetic and functional properties of beech, balsa, Scots pine and Parana pine

| Timber | Type | Aesthetic properties | Functional properties | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Beech** | Hardwood | Pale cream to pinkish-brown; fine, close, straight grain; smooth finish | Hard, very tough and strong; resists wear; good for turning and steam bending; not durable outdoors | Children's toys, chairs and furniture, kitchen utensils, workbenches, tool handles |
| **Balsa** | Hardwood | Very pale, almost white; open, even texture | Extremely light and soft; very easy to cut with a craft knife; low strength | Model making, prototypes, model aircraft, floats and buoyancy aids |
| **Scots pine** | Softwood | Pale cream-yellow with reddish-brown annual rings; often knotty | Fairly strong and light; easy to work; cheap and widely available; knots can weaken it and resin can seep | Construction timber, roof joists, budget furniture, shelving, DIY |
| **Parana pine** | Softwood | Pale with attractive red or brown streaks; straight grain and few knots | Hard and strong for a softwood; smooth finish; can warp if not well seasoned; more expensive than Scots pine | Interior joinery, staircases, built-in furniture, window frames |

<div class="exam-tip" markdown="1">
Balsa is a favourite exam trap. It is a **hardwood** because of the tree it comes from, even though it is the softest and lightest timber on this list.
</div>

## Properties, strengths and weaknesses of plywood and MDF

### Plywood

Plywood is made from thin layers of wood called **veneers** (or plies), glued together with the grain of each layer at **90°** to the one before. There is always an **odd number** of layers, so the grain on both outer faces runs the same way and the board stays balanced.

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Strong in all directions because of the crossed grain | Edges show the layers and need covering or finishing |
| Very stable — resists warping and splitting | The face veneer is thin and can be sanded through |
| Available in exterior and marine grades that resist water | Better grades are expensive |
| Can be bent into curves (flexible plywood) | Can splinter when cut |

**Uses:** furniture, shelving, drawer bottoms, boat building, flooring, laser-cut products.

### MDF (medium density fibreboard)

MDF is made from fine **wood fibres** mixed with resin adhesive and pressed into sheets under heat and pressure.

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| Smooth, flat surface on both sides — ideal for painting and veneering | Absorbs water and swells badly unless sealed |
| No grain, so it cuts and shapes evenly in any direction | Heavy for its size |
| Cheap and stable | Edges are porous and hard to finish without sealing |
| Machines and laser cuts cleanly with crisp edges | Screws hold poorly in the edges |
| | Fine dust when cut is a health hazard — use extraction and a mask |

**Uses:** flat-pack furniture, cabinets and kitchen units, display boards, moulds and formers, laser-cut parts.

## Physical and working properties of timber

| Property | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Toughness** | The ability to absorb impact without breaking | Beech is tough, so it is used for mallets and children's toys |
| **Flexibility** | The ability to bend without breaking and return to shape | Thin plywood and some steamed hardwoods bend into curves |
| **Strength** | The ability to withstand a force without breaking — timber is strongest **along** the grain | Scots pine joists carry floor loads |
| **Absorbency** | How readily a material soaks up moisture | MDF is very absorbent; marine plywood resists water |
| **Surface finish** | How smooth a surface can be made, and how well it takes a finish | Beech and Parana pine finish smoothly; MDF takes paint well once sealed |
| **Colour** | The natural colour and figure of the timber | Pale beech suits a Scandinavian look; Parana pine has decorative streaks |

<div class="key-term" markdown="1">
**Working properties** — how easily a material can be cut, shaped, joined and finished using tools and machines.
</div>
