## Why surface treatments and finishes are applied

<div class="key-term" markdown="1">
**Surface treatment** — anything applied to, or done to, the surface of a material to protect it, improve its appearance, or both.
</div>

Finishes have two jobs, and a good choice usually does both.

**Prolonging material life (functional):**

- **Metals** — ferrous metals **rust** when exposed to air and moisture; a finish seals the surface so oxygen and water cannot reach it.
- **Timbers** — wood absorbs moisture, so it swells, warps, rots and stains. A finish seals it and can protect against insects, fungi and UV light.
- **Polymers** — most do not corrode, but UV light can make them fade and go brittle, and scratches spoil clear surfaces.
- Finishes also make products **hygienic and easy to clean**, more **hard-wearing**, and can add **grip** or **insulation**.

**Improving aesthetic appeal:**

- Adds **colour**, which can match a brand, a colour scheme or a target market.
- Changes **texture** and **sheen** — high gloss, satin or matt.
- Enhances or hides the **natural features** of a material, such as wood grain.

**Preparation comes first.** A finish is only as good as the surface beneath it. Metals must be cleaned of rust, grease and scale (with emery cloth, wire wool or degreaser); timber must be sanded smooth with progressively finer abrasive paper and the dust removed. Some finishes need the surface **keyed** (lightly roughened) so they grip.

## Metal surface treatments: powder and plastic coating, paint and primer

| Finish | How it is applied | Benefits | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Powder coating** | Dry polymer powder is sprayed on with an electrostatic charge so it clings to the metal, then **baked in an oven** so it melts into a hard, even coat | Tough, chip-resistant and durable; wide range of colours and textures; no solvents, and overspray can be reused | Bicycle frames, garden furniture, car wheels, radiators, appliance casings |
| **Plastic (dip) coating** | The metal is heated (about 200°C) and dipped into a **fluidised bed** of polythene powder, which melts onto it; it is then returned to the oven to cure | Thick, soft, warm and grippy; good electrical and thermal insulation; protects against corrosion | Tool handles, dish racks, wire baskets, garden tools, playground equipment |
| **Paint and primer** | A **primer** is applied first, then one or more top coats by brush, roller or spray | Cheap and simple; any colour; easy to touch up | Gates, railings, machinery, vehicles, school metalwork |

<div class="key-term" markdown="1">
**Primer** — the first coat applied to bare material. It seals the surface, prevents corrosion (on metal) and helps the top coat stick. Ferrous metal needs a **metal primer**; paint applied straight onto bare steel will soon flake and rust underneath.
</div>

## Protecting natural and manufactured timbers

| Finish | How it works | Benefits and drawbacks | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Varnish** (e.g. polyurethane) | A clear or tinted coating that dries into a hard protective layer; applied in thin coats, lightly sanded between coats | Hard-wearing and waterproof; gloss, satin or matt; shows the grain. Can chip, and is hard to repair invisibly | Floors, tables, chairs, doors, children's toys |
| **Wax** | Rubbed into the surface and buffed to a soft sheen | Enhances the grain and feels natural; easy to apply and renew. Offers little protection against water or heat and needs reapplying | Fine furniture, turned bowls, wooden handles |
| **Paint** | Applied over a primer and undercoat, in one or more top coats | Any colour; hides the grain and defects; protects well. Hides the natural beauty of the timber | Children's furniture, MDF products, skirting boards, outdoor joinery |
| **Sealants** | A **sanding sealer** soaks into and seals the pores of the wood before varnish, wax or paint | Fewer top coats are needed and the finish goes on evenly | Bare timber and MDF before finishing |
| **Primers** | Seal bare wood so paint does not soak in, and help the top coat stick | Essential on MDF, whose cut edges are very absorbent | All painted timber and manufactured boards |

**Natural timber** is often given a clear finish (varnish or wax) to show off its grain. **Manufactured boards** such as MDF and chipboard have no attractive grain, so they are usually **painted**, **veneered** or covered with a **laminate** (e.g. melamine on kitchen worktops). Outdoor timber needs a **preservative** or exterior-grade finish to resist rot and insects.

<div class="exam-tip" markdown="1">
For a "describe the stages" question, give them in order: sand smooth with progressively finer grades, remove dust, apply sealer or primer, lightly sand, then apply thin top coats, sanding lightly between each.
</div>

## The self-finishing nature of plastics

<div class="key-term" markdown="1">
**Self-finishing** — a material that needs no applied finish, because it comes out of the manufacturing process with its final colour, texture and surface.
</div>

Many thermoforming and thermosetting plastics are self-finishing:

- **Colour runs all the way through** — pigment is added to the polymer before it is moulded, so scratches do not reveal a different colour underneath and there is no paint to chip off.
- **The mould gives the surface** — a polished mould produces a high-gloss product; a textured mould produces a matt, leather-effect or grip pattern. Every product made in that mould has the same finish.
- **They do not corrode** — so they need no protective coating against water.

This saves time, money and materials in manufacture, and is one reason injection-moulded products are so cheap in high volumes.

**When plastics do need finishing:** parts cut from stock sheet have rough edges, which are finished by **draw filing**, **wet-and-dry paper** (used wet), **polishing compound** or **flame polishing** (acrylic). Plastics can also be **painted** (with a suitable primer), **printed**, **vinyl-decorated** or **laser-engraved** for decoration or branding.
